# What Rizom delivers, and why it matters

Every organisation runs on knowledge that lives in people rather than in systems: who knows what, whose judgment settles which questions, how information actually moves. It is rarely written down, because it always worked. It stops working when a key person leaves, a team grows fast, or an AI tool is asked to do a job nobody ever specified.

Rizom makes that knowledge system visible, and then helps it work.

## What we do

We sit with the people who hold the knowledge, one at a time, and ask two questions: what do you know that nobody else here does, and what goes wrong if you are not here tomorrow. The answers are rarely what the org chart says.

Then we put the team in a room and have them draw their own map: who knows what, whose judgment counts where, how information moves. We facilitate; they draw. It is usually the first time everyone has looked at the same picture.

We write down what the map shows, and what to change: a handful of adjustments to roles, rituals and tooling, in order of what is cheapest to do and what matters most. That document belongs to the organisation.

Where an organisation wants the map to stay alive, rather than decay again the next time someone leaves, we build that with them: a shared memory their people and their AI tools draw on in daily work, captured from where work already happens, open source and on their own infrastructure. That is a separate step, and only some organisations need it.

The method is transactive memory systems: forty years of research on how teams share knowledge without everyone needing to know everything.

## Why this leads to innovation

New work is mostly recombination of what people in the organisation already know. A team can only recombine what it can see. Where nobody knows who knows what, or whose judgment settles a question, every new initiative routes through the same two or three people and stalls when they are busy or gone. Where the map exists and is shared, the team takes on new things without waiting for them. Teams with a strong shared map of expertise outperform teams of equal individual talent, and the difference is structural, not personal, which is why it can be diagnosed and repaired.

AI is the current test of this. An AI tool is a new team member with none of the map: it does not know who to defer to, what is settled, or what is context. This is why rollouts underdeliver. It looks like an AI problem. It is the absence of a knowledge system, made visible.

## Why it is not a technical exercise

The map is made by people, in the room. The fragile part is not the data but the trust: whose judgment is accepted on which questions, and whether that is agreed or merely assumed. No tool produces that. Software can only keep a map current once it exists. That is why the first steps contain no software at all, and why the build only makes sense once the organisational work has been done.

## Who we work with

Organisations where a small team carries a lot of knowledge: institutions, programmes, companies. The moment is usually one of these. Someone who carries a lot is leaving, or has just left. The team has grown faster than its habits. An AI tool was given a job and nobody can say why it gets it wrong. Our counterpart is whoever owns how the work flows: a director, a head of operations, a programme lead.

Rizom runs on what it describes: rizom.ai is itself such a memory, and the platform is open source on GitHub.
