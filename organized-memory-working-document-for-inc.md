# Organized Memory

---

*Working document, August 2026 — unfinished on purpose.*

**1.** The institutions that housed critical thought are shedding it. A mandatory retirement here, a defunded lectoraat there, a humanities department quietly closed — the pattern is continental, and it is accelerating. Within a decade, most of the communities that produced Europe's critical knowledge of networks will have no institutional home. Each will face the same question: where does intellectual life live when the institution is gone?

**2.** The available answers are platforms, and they are not answers. A Discord, a newsletter, a shared drive: dispersal into individual precarity, on infrastructure built to monetize exactly that. The critique of this condition is complete — it has been complete for years, and much of it was written in Amsterdam. What was never available is an alternative that does not require the institution back.

**3.** Twenty years ago the question of organized networks was posed: how loose association becomes durable form without hardening into institution. The concept was never the problem. The problem was that there was nothing to organize *on* except the platforms themselves — the medium contradicted the message.

**4.** The claim of this text: the missing layer is memory. An institution, reduced to its actual function, is a memory that outlives any individual's attention — the archive, the record of decisions, what was published, who said what. Platforms perform this function by centralizing that memory and renting it back. The alternative is memory that stays where it is made. Each person holds their own: archive, notes, published work, and a machine that reads and writes alongside them — small, situated, theirs. These memories find each other directly and cite each other by name. The collective is composed by people who choose to connect, and recomposes when they choose differently. Organized networks, standing on organized memory.

**5.** This is no longer speculative. The infrastructure exists in first form — self-hosted, open licensed, plain text, no platform underneath — and the interesting questions have moved from whether it can be built to what a community becomes when it runs on it.

**6.** Which is why what happens to a research institute in Amsterdam this September may be read two ways. As an ending: zero staff, zero budget, the institution gone. Or as the first experiment in the condition this text describes. A roof can be found — hospitality is what this city's scene has always done well — but a roof is not a form. The community keeps its domain, its archive, its network, and the question the building cannot answer: what holds it together now. Twenty-two years of theorising networks, testing whether its own argument holds.

## Concretely

From September, INC could run its own brain. A brain, concretely, is three things at once: a conversational interface where the team asks questions and gets answers drawn from everything it holds; a growing plain-text collection of notes, decisions, and drafts that everyone can read and add to; and, where wanted, a public face — pages, feeds — generated from the same content. Ask it *what did we decide about the publishing plans, and why* — it answers, with sources. Mine has been running publicly for a year: **yeehaa.io**. The fastest way to understand this offer is to look at it — or I'll walk you, or anyone who's curious, through it in half an hour.

For INC it would be the shared working memory of a distributed community — the record of decisions, projects, and publications from day one of INC 2.0; the connective tissue a mailing list used to be; community memory in practice rather than as ambition.

We would host and operate it. Free, live this autumn, on infrastructure INC can leave at any moment — everything lives in plain, portable text, and the exit path is documented and tested, not promised. It touches nothing that works: EtherPort, VOID, the website, the library stay exactly where they are and whose they are. The roof houses; this remembers.

In parallel, we are testing several funding routes in which INC would participate as a full partner with its own workpackage and budget. More on those as they firm up — none of this depends on them.

Nothing here requires a decision now, or work from anyone.

## Open

From here, this can only be written from inside. What stays open:

— what this community actually is, once "institute" no longer names it: members, milieu, readership, something else;
— what holds a distributed community together when the mailing list is gone;
— what the generation that has only known the crippled version of social media would do with infrastructure of its own;
— what refusal means when the machine is yours;
— what this is called.

*This is where the draft stops. The second half is not mine to write.*
