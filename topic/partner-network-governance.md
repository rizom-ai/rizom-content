---
title: Partner Certification and Network Governance
---
A governance and operating framework for scaling an open platform through a certified network of independent practitioners and institutionally aligned partners. Partners retain their own businesses, capabilities, infrastructure, and self-hosted access while delivering under a shared methodology, playbooks, tools, branding, public registry, referrals, and co-delivery opportunities—expanding implementation capacity and distribution without employment, exclusivity, guaranteed work, client ownership, proprietary software, or platform lock-in.

Certification is earned through apprenticeship and co-delivery on real paid engagements, enabling demonstrated capability, practice validation, and case-study development rather than relying solely on a one-time examination. A unified practitioner track can use capability tags and public standing states such as candidate, active, and steward. Only active listed partners may use certification marks, with dormancy affecting visibility without automatically ending participation. A defined share of engagement revenue may support the commons and platform stewardship, subject to governance and legal review.

The framework should establish eligibility, agreements, certification standards, capability tags, engagement records, delivery and interoperability requirements, published commercial terms or price bands, evidence sharing, client commitments, accountability, trademark use, registry publication, revenue allocation, disputes, remediation, appeals, and fair delisting. A public, machine-readable registry communicates partner status and capabilities, while the foundation or designated marks holder oversees certification, standards, trademark rights, listings, enforcement, and institutional representation. Open-source licensing can help prevent proprietary capture.

Governance must preserve shared authorship and legitimate representation, particularly where an established intellectual or community network serves as an institutional partner. Partners should retain meaningful control over how the relationship is represented publicly and should not be reduced to a platform-led marketing channel. Board or foundation representation, a shared founding text, and explicit commitments to partner voice, lineage, and standing can provide accountability. Partner adoption, successful delivery, reference deployments, institutional demand, community uptake, and expansion into additional communities are key tests of whether the model is genuinely transferable beyond founder-led delivery.
