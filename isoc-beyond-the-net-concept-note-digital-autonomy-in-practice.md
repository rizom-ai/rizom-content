# Digital Autonomy in Practice

A possible Beyond the Net project for the Internet Society Netherlands Chapter.

This is a sketch, not a submission. It describes a project the chapter could run and asks the board a single question: is this worth developing into a real application? Everything below is meant to be argued with. The numbers are indicative and the scope is deliberately small.

## The situation

Dutch civil-society organisations keep their institutional memory on platforms they do not control. Digital rights groups, community archives, local media, small cultural and research institutes: their archives, their decisions, their published work all sit in systems owned by someone else, on terms that can change without notice.

There is an awkwardness here that this chapter is better placed than most to name. The organisations that argue hardest for an open internet are frequently the ones most tied to closed infrastructure. For a long time it is cheap and it works, which is why nobody examines it. Then a grant ends, or the two people who knew where everything was kept move on, or an institution restructures, and the memory leaves with the account.

We have one such case in view already. A research institute that spent two decades writing about platform dependency has just left its university host. Twenty years of publications, event recordings and working archive sit on systems it no longer controls, and it now has to stand up as an independent organisation from scratch. For them this is not a hypothetical.

We are not naming it here. Nothing has been agreed with them, and the board should decide whether it likes the idea before it decides anything about a particular organisation.

## What the project would do

The chapter selects three to five organisations from its network and moves each one onto open-source infrastructure it owns outright: its archive and working material in plain, portable formats, on its own domain, with a written and tested way to leave. Technical work is bought in from an external partner. Selection, coordination and everything published stay with the chapter.

Three to five is a deliberate number. It is small enough that each organisation gets real attention and nothing has to be rushed, and varied enough that what we write at the end is about a method rather than a single lucky outcome. A larger group would look more ambitious on paper and would be worse in practice.

Over twelve months:

- The chapter opens a call and selects participants, in the first two months.
- Each organisation is migrated and supported, staggered across months two to eight, then run and maintained through to the end.
- Two public sessions, open to the wider chapter community, on what digital autonomy costs and requires in practice.
- One participant works through a full exit: moving off the infrastructure this project set up for them, to prove the door opens. If the institute described above takes part, nothing has to be simulated, since their arrival is already an exit from somewhere else.
- A written account, published by the chapter, of what worked, what was harder than expected, and what did not survive the move.

That last item is the actual deliverable. Very little of this is written down anywhere a small organisation could use. Universities across Europe are restructuring and cutting institutes loose, and the question is not going to stay rare.

## Why this belongs to the chapter

Digital autonomy is something the chapter already argues for in public. This is the same argument at the layer where its own community works, with the outcome written down rather than asserted.

It can also become a vendor pilot wearing a chapter's name, which is what the structure below is meant to prevent.

Selection sits with the board, not the technical partner. The written account is published by the chapter, under its own name and an open licence, and it stays honest about failures or it is not worth publishing. The method is documented so that other chapters can reuse it without needing us or any particular supplier. The technical work is purchased; the project is not.

## Who does the work

Coordination is real work and should not quietly land on a volunteer. Running an open call, selecting participants, keeping a handful of organisations moving and organising two public sessions is somewhere around a day a week for a year. The budget pays for that explicitly, either as a part-time coordinator or as compensation for whoever on the board takes it on. If the board is not comfortable with that line, the project is too big and should shrink further.

## Indicative budget

| Line | USD |
|---|---|
| Migration, hosting and support (external partner) | 17,000 |
| Chapter coordination and selection | 7,000 |
| Writing and publishing the account | 5,000 |
| Two public sessions | 4,000 |
| Contingency | 2,000 |
| **Total** | **35,000** |

Just under half goes to the external partner; the majority sits with the chapter. The figure is above the USD 20,000 threshold at which Beyond the Net requires a named external partner, which is why the partnership is set out below rather than left implicit.

## After the twelve months

Each organisation ends up owning its own instance, with no licence fee during the project or after it. What remains is a modest hosting bill, in the order of tens of euros a month, which participants take over at the end. Packaging the software so that this is genuinely cheap to self-host is being funded separately, through an NLnet Open Internet Stack application. The software is AGPL and the exit path is documented, so an organisation that wants to move elsewhere, or run the whole thing itself, can do so without asking permission.

## The proposed partner

The external partner would be Stichting Rizom, a Dutch foundation that develops and maintains Rizom Brains, the open-source platform this project would deploy. 

Rizom B.V., the commercial entity with the same will not be involved in any form. The two entities are completely independent, and this project will fully fall under the responsibility of the stichting and its statutes.


## What we are asking of the board

1. Is this a project the chapter wants? A view in principle is enough at this stage.
2. If yes, appoint a board contact to work on it.
3. Authorise a full application to be drafted for a later meeting, with the scope, the partner and the budget open to revision.

Nothing here is fixed. The parts most worth arguing about are the number of participants, how much of the budget the chapter holds, and whether the technical partner should be chosen competitively.
