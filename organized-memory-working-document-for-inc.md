# Organized Memory

**Context.** Working document drafted 2026-08-10 for Geert Lovink (INC).

**1.** The institutions that housed critical thought are shedding it. A mandatory retirement here, a defunded lectoraat there, a humanities department quietly closed — the pattern is continental, and it is accelerating. Within a decade, most of the communities that produced Europe's critical knowledge of networks will have no institutional home. Each will face the same question: where does intellectual life live when the institution is gone?

**2.** The available answers are platforms, and they are not answers. A Discord, a newsletter, a shared drive: dispersal into individual precarity, on infrastructure built to monetize exactly that. The critique of this condition is complete — it has been complete for years, and much of it was written in Amsterdam. What was never available is an alternative that does not require the institution back.

**3.** Twenty years ago the question of organized networks was posed: how loose association becomes durable form without hardening into institution. The concept was never the problem. The problem was that there was nothing to organize *on* except the platforms themselves — the medium contradicted the message.

**4.** The claim of this text: the missing layer is memory. An institution, reduced to its actual function, is a memory that outlives any individual's attention — the archive, the record of decisions, what was published, who said what. Platforms perform this function by centralizing that memory and renting it back. The alternative is memory that stays where it is made. Each person holds their own: archive, notes, published work, and a machine that reads and writes alongside them — small, situated, theirs. These memories find each other directly and cite each other by name. The collective is composed by people who choose to connect, and recomposes when they choose differently. Organized networks, standing on organized memory.

**5.** Which is why what happens to a research institute in Amsterdam this September may be read two ways. As an ending: zero staff, zero budget, the institution gone. Or as the first experiment in the condition this text describes. A roof can be found — hospitality is what this city's scene has always done well — but a roof is not a form. The community keeps its domain, its archive, its network, and the question the building cannot answer: what holds it together now. Twenty-two years of theorising networks, testing whether its own argument holds.

## Open

From here, this can only be written from inside. What stays open:

— what this community actually is, once "institute" no longer names it: members, milieu, readership, something else;
— what holds a distributed community together when the mailing list is gone;
— what the generation that has only known the crippled version of social media would do with infrastructure of its own;
— what refusal means when the machine is yours;
— what this is called.
